# Theoretical eye-color change without contacts

A private research concept exploring whether eye color could ever be changed without contact lenses, including the speculative question of whether genetic or gene-regulatory changes could alter iris pigmentation in an adult.

## Research status

This is a hypothetical, literature-based inquiry. It does not describe an available treatment. No established clinical method uses DNA editing to cosmetically change the color of a healthy adult's eyes.

## Established background

Iris color is related to melanin in the front layers of the iris. Multiple genes contribute to natural variation, so eye color is not a simple one-gene switch. Genetic associations explain parts of how eye color develops; they do not show that editing an adult iris would safely or predictably change its color.

In the United States, the FDA-approved CustomFlex artificial iris is indicated for full or partial aniridia and related iris defects. Its manufacturer says it is not intended for cosmetic eye-color changes in a normal eye.

## Hypothetical question

Could a future, highly controlled change in pigment regulation in an adult iris alter its visible color while preserving normal vision?

This is a research question, not a proposed intervention. A conceptual model could examine whether changing pigment production in existing iris cells would be sufficient, how adult tissue differs from pigmentation established during development, and whether any color shift could be uniform, stable, and safe. These are unknowns; this project makes no claim that they can currently be solved.

## Questions to explore

- What biological processes and genes contribute to normal iris pigmentation?
- Which findings describe development, and which apply to an already-developed adult iris?
- What would have to be true for a theoretical color change to be predictable, uniform, durable, and reversible?
- What vision, inflammation, tissue-damage, and long-term risks would have to be ruled out?
- What evidence and regulatory review would be required before a cosmetic use could be considered?

## Research plan

1. Review high-level literature on iris pigmentation and eye-color genetics.
2. Separate established observations, evidence from disease research, theoretical extrapolations, and unsupported claims.
3. Build a qualitative feasibility matrix covering biological plausibility, evidence gaps, safety questions, and ethical concerns.
4. Track clinical evidence and regulatory indications for existing eye-color procedures, without treating them as recommendations.

## Scope and safety

This repository is for theory, literature review, and non-clinical conceptual analysis. It is not medical advice or a development plan. It will not include human gene-editing instructions, target sequences, protocols, delivery methods, sourcing, or instructions for eye surgery, lasers, or self-experimentation.

For a temporary appearance change without touching the eye, nonmedical options such as lighting, eyewear styling, or photo previews can be considered; these do not change iris pigment.
